People often call it sugar waxing. It is an easy name to remember, but it is not quite right, because there is no wax in it at all. The paste is made from sugar, water and lemon, and people have used it to remove hair for a very long time.

Here is a short look at where sugaring is said to come from, how it has changed, and why so many people still choose it today.

## Where sugaring is said to begin

Sugaring is usually described as one of the oldest ways to remove hair. The story most often told is that it began in ancient Egypt and spread across Persia and the wider Middle East. You will often read that Cleopatra used a sugar paste. It is a lovely story, but there is little firm evidence for it, so we treat it as legend rather than fact.

What we can say is that sugaring has deep roots in the Middle East and North Africa. In many Arabic-speaking countries it is known as halawa, from the word for sweet. For generations it was a home skill. The paste was cooked in the kitchen from ingredients most families already had, and the method was passed on from one generation to the next.

That is part of its charm. Sugar, water and lemon are simple, cheap and easy to find. Long before razors and hair removal creams, a paste you could make at home was a practical answer to an everyday question.

## Two ways to use the paste

Because it was learned by hand, sugaring has always varied from place to place. Today there are two main ways to use it.

The **hand technique** uses a thicker paste. It is spread over the skin, then flicked off with the hand, taking the hair with it. This is the method most people mean when they say sugaring, and it is how we work at SISI Clinic.

The **strip method** uses a softer paste that is lifted away with cloth strips, a little like strip wax. It is quicker to learn, which is one reason the name sugar waxing caught on.

Over time sugaring travelled well beyond the Middle East. It is now very popular in Eastern Europe, and more and more studios in the UK offer it too.

## From kitchen to studio

The basic recipe has hardly changed. What has changed is the way it is made and used.

Professional pastes are cooked to a consistent texture, so they behave the same way every time. Some are softer and some are firmer, which lets a therapist choose the right one for the area and the warmth of the room.

The technique has become more precise too. At our studio, a treatment works like this:

- The skin is cleansed and dusted lightly, so the paste grips the hair rather than the skin.
- The warm paste, at around body temperature, is worked into the skin against the direction of hair growth.
- It is then flicked off in the direction the hair grows, taking the hair out from the root.
- We work in small sections and check for stray hairs at the end.

Hygiene has moved on as well. We use single-use tools and fresh disposable gloves for every client.

## Why the name sugar waxing stuck

Most people in the UK grew up with waxing. When they first heard about sugaring, it was natural to think of it as a kind of wax. The name stuck, and many people still search for sugar waxing today.

The two are different, though. Wax is usually heated much more, and it tends to be pulled off against the direction of hair growth. Sugar paste is used warm, and it removes hair in the direction it grows. We explain the differences in more detail in [Is sugaring the same as waxing?](/is-sugaring-the-same-as-waxing/)

## Why people choose sugaring today

The reasons are much the same as they have always been, with a few modern ones added.

- **Simple ingredients.** You know what goes on your skin: sugar, water and lemon.
- **Warm, not hot.** The paste is used at around body temperature, so there is no hot wax on your skin.
- **Kind to sensitive skin.** Because the paste grips the hair rather than the skin, many people with sensitive skin find it easier to tolerate than waxing.
- **Easy clean-up.** The paste dissolves in water, so any residue simply washes off.
- **Smooth for weeks.** Hair is removed from the root, so results usually last three to five weeks, depending on how fast your hair grows.
- **May help reduce ingrown hairs.** Removing hair in the direction it grows may mean fewer hairs break under the skin. Read more in our guide to [sugaring and ingrown hairs](/sugaring-hair-removal-ingrown-hairs/).
